What a sauna is

A sauna is a small room heated well above ordinary room temperature, used in short sessions of dry or steamy heat. Two kinds turn up almost everywhere: traditional, heated by a stove and a pile of hot stones, and infrared, where panels warm your body directly. Both are covered here.

Trouble in a sauna is rarely the heat on its own. It is usually heat and something else: no water since breakfast, a drink at lunch, a warning sign sat through because the round was not over.

03

Two kinds of heat

Two rooms, two ways of getting hot, and one body that answers both in much the same way.

A stove heats the room and a pile of stones on top of it. The air itself gets hot, and it is usually dry until somebody adds water.

  • Ladle a little water onto the stones and you get löyly: the burst of steam that rises when water hits hot rock, and the wave of heat you feel a second later. Humidity climbs for a moment, then settles.
  • Heat stacks toward the ceiling, so the bench you choose changes the experience more than anything else in the room.
  • Ask before you pour. In a shared room, löyly is everybody's.

Infrared saunas use panels that warm your body directly rather than heating all of the air first, so the air stays cooler than it would in a traditional room.

  • The air can feel mild while you are still sweating steadily, which is the part people misread.
  • Cooler air is not the same as less demand. Your body is still warming up and still losing fluid.
  • There are no stones and no steam, so there is no löyly here.

The room is different. What your body does about it is broadly the same.

  • Your core temperature, meaning the temperature deep inside you rather than at the skin, rises. You flush, and you sweat.
  • You lose fluid whether or not the air feels wet.
  • The same habits carry across both: water first, short rounds, time out of the heat afterwards, and stopping when you feel done.

The heat is the point. Enduring it is not.

04

What the heat does

Sitting still in a hot room is not doing nothing. Open a section to read what is actually going on.

The blood vessels near your skin widen and your heart beats faster to push blood outward, because the skin is where heat leaves the body. The feeling has been compared to light exercise. Treat it as a real demand on your circulation rather than as rest with the heating on.

Sweat is the cooling system working. You lose water and salts while you sit, and in a dry room the sweat evaporates so fast that you underestimate how much has gone. Drink water before and after. If you are already thirsty, you are behind.

Warm tissue feels looser and more willing to move, which is why heat after training is pleasant and why easy stretching afterwards comes without a fight. Whether that speeds recovery in any measurable way is less settled than the marketing suggests. Enjoy it for how it feels and keep your expectations modest.

Many people find that heat followed by cooling leaves them calm and ready to sleep. Some of that is the fall in body temperature afterwards. Some of it is a stretch of quiet with nothing to do and nowhere to be, which is not nothing.

Long-running population studies in Finland, where sauna use is ordinary, have associated frequent use with better heart health. Association is not cause. People who use a sauna often may differ from people who do not in ways the study cannot separate out. Read it as encouraging, not as a prescription, and never as a reason to skip a conversation with your own clinician.

05

Who should check first

Seven situations where the sensible move is a question before a session. Select one to read why it is worth asking.

None of this is a diagnosis and none of it is a list of bans. It is a prompt to ask somebody who knows your history, before a first session rather than after one.

Pregnancy

Heat raises core body temperature, and raised core temperature is something clinicians watch carefully in pregnancy. Guidance varies by country and by stage. Ask your midwife or doctor rather than reading a forum, and ask before the first session rather than after it.

Heart conditions and blood pressure

Heat asks your heart and circulation to work: faster beats, wider vessels, and a blood pressure that can drop when you stand up. If you live with a heart condition, unstable blood pressure high or low, or you have had a recent cardiac event, this is a conversation with your clinician and not a guess on the bench.

Medications

Some medicines change how you regulate heat, how much you sweat, or how your blood pressure behaves when you stand. Diuretics, blood pressure medicines, sedatives and various others can all matter here. Your pharmacist can answer this in about a minute, and it is a good minute to spend.

Alcohol

Alcohol and heat both lower blood pressure and both dry you out, and together they make fainting considerably more likely. Do not drink before a session and do not drink during one. Water is the drink here, and it is not a compromise.

Children and older adults

Children heat up faster than adults and have far less practice noticing it or saying so. Older adults are more likely to be on medication and may feel a change in balance more sharply on standing. Both do better with shorter, cooler, supervised visits, and with somebody paying attention who is not also enjoying themselves.

Recent illness or a fever

If you already have a fever, your body is running hot deliberately and does not need the help. If you are recovering from an illness, dehydration and a lower tolerance for heat both linger longer than the symptoms do. Wait until you are properly well.

Open wounds or skin conditions

Heat, sweat and shared benches are hard on broken skin, and several skin conditions flare with heat rather than settle. Cover what can be covered, sit on your own towel either way, and ask a clinician about anything that is currently open or currently angry.

06

A good session, step by step

Nine steps in order. Most of what makes a session good happens before you sit down and after you get up.

Step 1 of 9

1

Drink water first

Go in already topped up. You are about to sweat, and starting behind is how a pleasant session turns into a headache in the car. Water, not alcohol, and not instead of a meal you skipped.

2

Rinse off

A quick warm shower before you go in takes the day off your skin. In a shared room it is also the ordinary courtesy. Dry yourself before you sit down: damp skin sweats less usefully.

3

Sit lower if you are new

Heat stacks toward the ceiling, so the lower bench is meaningfully gentler than the top one. There is no prize for the top bench. If this is your first time, do not go alone: bring someone, or at least tell someone you are in there.

4

Keep the first round short

Go in, sit, and pay attention. Come out while you still feel good, not when you have had enough. A short first round tells you what your body makes of this room, and you can always go back in.

5

Cool down properly

A cool-down is the deliberate stretch after a round where you leave the heat and let your temperature and heart rate come back down. A cool shower, fresh air, or simply sitting somewhere cooler all count. Skipping it is the most common mistake in the whole session.

6

Rest

Sit down for a while and let your breathing settle. This is not dead time between the good bits. The calm, loose feeling people go to saunas for mostly arrives here, off the bench.

7

Drink water again

Replace what you sweated out before you decide anything else. Plain water is fine. Alcohol is not a cool-down, whatever the room next door is selling.

8

A second round only if you feel good

If you feel steady, clear headed and comfortable, go back in. If you feel wrung out, light headed or vaguely off, you are finished for today and that is a fine result.

9

Stop when you feel done

No target, no count, nobody keeping score. The body gives you the signal well before a clock would. The whole skill is listening to it and then actually doing what it said.

Slow breathing

Optional. Four counts in, six counts out, which is the pace people fall into on the bench anyway.

Not running.

Motion is turned off in your display settings, so the breathing pacer is not shown. The pace it keeps is simple enough to do without it: breathe in slowly, then let the breath out for longer than you took it in.

07

Stay, cool down, or stop

Eight moments on the bench. For each one, choose whether you stay put, get out and cool down now, or stop and get help. You can change an answer, and nothing here is timed.

Situation 1 of 8

Answered 0 of 8.

1

You shift on the bench to stand up and the room tilts for a second before it settles.

2

A few minutes into your first round you feel warm and loose. Your breathing is easy and nothing hurts.

3

A dull headache is building behind your eyes. It was not there when you came in.

4

You felt sick in the heat and came out. You have been sitting in the cool for a while, you still feel sick, and your skin has gone clammy.

5

You are on the bench when you remember the two beers you had at lunch.

6

The top bench is hotter than you are enjoying. You feel fine otherwise, and the lower bench is free.

7

Your heart is thumping hard and it does not settle when you sit still and breathe slowly.

8

First visit. The air is fiercer than you expected, and you have not had anything to drink since this morning.
