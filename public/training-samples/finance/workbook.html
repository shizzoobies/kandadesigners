Reading the P&L

Return to the interactive course

Reference workbook. All figures describe an invented two-location bakery using accrual accounting. This simplified statement omits income tax. This demonstration does not replace advice from your accountant.

The statement, top to bottom

LineAmount
Revenue$80,000
Cost of goods sold−$32,000
Gross profit$48,000
Operating expenses−$43,000
Operating income$5,000
Loan interest−$900
Net income$4,100

Revenue records everything sold before costs, including invoiced sales not yet collected. Cost of goods sold is the cost of the products sold: ingredients, packaging and production wages here. Production-wage treatment can differ between businesses; compare using a consistent policy.

Gross profit is revenue less cost of goods sold. Operating expenses pay for running the shops: rent and utilities $14,000; counter and management payroll $22,000; marketing and delivery $3,000; insurance, repairs and supplies $4,000. Operating income measures the trade before interest and non-operating items. One-off gains can improve the bottom line without improving the trade.

Interest is the charge for borrowing. Principal repayment is not an expense. Net income is the profit after the costs shown, not the change in bank balance.

Margin makes the months comparable

Gross margin = gross profit ÷ revenue × 100. This month: $48,000 ÷ $80,000 = 60%. Next month, a discounted wholesale order increases revenue to $92,000 and gross profit to $50,600. Gross margin is now 55%. Dollars rose, but the bakery kept less gross profit per sales dollar. The change is five percentage points, not a five percent relative change.

Move the levers

At baseline, $80,000 sales less 40% COGS less $43,000 operating expenses gives $5,000 operating income. Increase COGS to 45% with everything else fixed: $80,000 − $36,000 − $43,000 = $1,000. A $4,000 cost change reduces operating income dollar for dollar.

In the interactive model, price changes sales revenue. Volume changes revenue and production cost. Operating expenses and interest remain fixed. The model illustrates arithmetic; it does not forecast demand. A price increase can affect actual sales volume.

Where the ten transactions belong

  • Ingredients and boxes used in sold products: cost of goods sold.
  • Store manager's salary: operating expense.
  • $18,000 oven purchase: equipment asset. Depreciation, not the entire purchase, reaches expense over its useful life.
  • Rent: operating expense.
  • $1,400 loan principal repayment: reduces cash and debt. Interest is recorded separately on the P&L.
  • $3,000 owner draw: equity transaction, not an expense. A salary for management work would be different.
  • Delivery fuel: operating expense under this bakery's policy. Other consistent policies may differ.
  • Counter sales: revenue before costs.
  • Card processing fee: operating expense here, shown separately from sales.
  • Unsold bread: inventory. Its cost reaches COGS when sold. Spoilage is written off when recognized.

Reconcile profit and cash

The cafes owe $9,000, invoiced on day 5 and collected on day 50. Revenue is recorded before the cash arrives. The bakery also spent $6,000 on flour, all still in inventory at month end. Its cost reaches COGS as the resulting bread is sold. Annual insurance cost $7,200 cash but only $600 is this month's expense; $6,600 is prepaid for future months.

Subtract these timing differences from net income: $4,100 − $9,000 − $6,000 − $6,600 = −$17,500 cash movement. This assumes no other cash movements and is not a complete cash flow statement.

The expansion decision

The owner proposes a third shop needing $60,000 of build-out spending, mostly in the first sixty days. Last month's $4,100 profit alone does not justify signing. Review gross margin across three months by location, forecast collections and payments, and prepare a schedule of build-out spending before committing. A promised $8,000 operating-cost cut or an assumed price increase is not a tested funding plan.

Close the books

The three calculation answers are: gross margin 60%; largest operating expense $22,000 counter and management payroll; operating income after COGS rises to 45% $1,000.

For your next review, check the period and accounting policy. Read revenue as a trend. Compare gross margin. Inspect major operating expenses as a share of revenue. Separate operating income from one-off gains. Ask what sits in receivables, inventory and prepayments, then read the cash flow statement.
