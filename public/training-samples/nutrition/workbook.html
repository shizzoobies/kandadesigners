Welcome

Good food. Less overthinking.

Build a meal by eye. Find what matters on a label. Leave with one small change that fits your life. The interactive workshop takes about eight minutes; this workbook is yours to use at any pace.

What you will practice

  • Use a divided plate as a flexible guide.
  • Place eight everyday foods and explain their role.
  • Explore six label details and scale the numbers to the amount.
  • Find everyday sources of protein and fiber.
  • Notice context around eating without diagnosing feelings.
  • Choose one practical swap to keep.

A registered dietitian or clinician can help with individual needs, health conditions, pregnancy, medication or allergies. Nothing here is a personalized diet plan.

The plate guide

A guide, not a rulebook.

The proportions are a starting point, not a prescription for portion size. Think about the ingredients across a meal, whether it is on a plate, in a bowl, in soup or shared at a table.

Vegetables and fruit: roughly half

Make room for color. Fresh, frozen, canned and cooked versions count. Combine what you enjoy with what you can afford.

Protein: roughly a quarter

Eggs, fish, poultry, beans, lentils and tofu are options. Protein supports growth and repair. Plant choices such as beans also bring fiber.

Grains and starch: the other quarter

Whole grains retain the bran and germ. Think oats, brown rice and whole-wheat bread. This workshop also puts starchy vegetables in this section; different plate guides classify them differently.

Beside the plate

Add a drink and the fats or flavorings you enjoy. Water is an easy default. Dairy or fortified alternatives can fit your wider eating pattern. Adjust for your needs, culture and preferences.

Label investigation

Turn the package around.

Honey Oat Crunch is a fictional cereal used for practice. The teaching label below is abbreviated, not a complete commercial Nutrition Facts panel.

Honey Oat Crunch: teaching label
Servings per container8
Serving size¾ cup (40g)
Calories160
Dietary fiber3g
Total sugars12g, including 9g added sugars
Other example amountsProtein 4g, sodium 210mg, total fat 3g
IngredientsWhole-grain oats, sugar, rice flour, honey, canola oil, salt, natural flavor.

The six details

Servings per container
The whole box is not one serving. This box contains eight reference servings.
Serving size
The numbers describe three quarters of a cup. Serving size reflects typical consumption, not advice about how much to eat.
Calories
An energy amount per serving, not a food score. Nutrients and enjoyment matter too.
Dietary fiber
Compare similar products at similar serving sizes.
Added sugars
The 9 grams are included in the 12 grams of total sugars. Do not add them together.
Ingredient order
Ingredients appear in descending order by weight. Whole-grain oats come first here. For allergies, always check the complete package.

Change the amount

What would happen if you ate two labeled servings? Double the energy and nutrient amounts. The same idea works for half a serving.

Look inside the meal

Protein and fiber do different jobs.

Protein: build and repair

Your body uses protein to build and repair tissues. Eggs, fish, meat, dairy, tofu, beans and lentils offer different ways to include it. Try beans in a bowl, yogurt with oats or an egg with toast. There is no single food you have to eat.

Fiber: more plant possibilities

Fiber is a carbohydrate in plant foods that is not fully digested. Whole grains, beans, fruit, vegetables, nuts and seeds are sources. Increase fiber gradually if you are adding more, and include fluids. What feels comfortable varies from person to person.

One pantry overlap

Beans bring both protein and fiber. Keep a can you enjoy on hand and add it to soup, a grain bowl or a simple lunch.

Hunger, habit and context

Notice the moment.

Hunger, thirst, mood, tiredness and routine can overlap. A clue helps you pause; it does not tell you what you must feel. You do not need to pass a test before eating.

The snack drawer

You pass the printer and open the snack drawer without thinking. You are not sure whether you want food.

A familiar place can prompt an automatic routine. Pause and notice what you want. You can still choose a snack.

A busy morning

Your morning has been nonstop. You notice you have barely paused to drink.

That is useful information about your routine. Check in with thirst and make water available. It does not tell you whether you also need food.

After a short night

You feel distracted after a night with very little sleep. Food is on your mind.

Sleep, appetite and mood can interact. Notice the context without deciding hunger must be false. Food and rest can both be appropriate.

Lunch has slipped

A meeting runs over. You realize several hours have passed since breakfast. Lunch sounds appealing.

Time since eating can be useful information. Hunger feels different across people and days.

The film and the bowl

The film starts. Your hand reaches for the bowl as soon as it lands beside you.

The setting may be part of the routine. Notice taste, enjoyment and how you feel, rather than labeling the snack as a failure.

Back from a walk

You return from a warm afternoon walk and want something cool to drink.

Make time to cool down and notice your needs. This exercise cannot diagnose symptoms or decide whether food, fluids, rest or medical care are needed.
