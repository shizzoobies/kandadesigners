Before your first set

Strength training is not a talent you either have or you do not. It is a skill: a handful of shapes your body already makes every day, practised on purpose, with a load.

Strength training
Working your muscles against a load, which can be your own body weight, a dumbbell, a band, or a machine. The load is what gives muscle and bone a reason to get stronger.
Rep
Short for repetition. One complete movement, down and back up once.
Set
A group of reps done back to back, then a rest.

Keyboard. Tab moves between controls, Enter or Space chooses, and the left and right arrows move between tabs. The sliders take the arrow keys. Nothing is timed and there is no sound.

This is general education, not medical advice and not a personal training program. If you have an injury, a heart condition, are pregnant, or have ever been told to avoid exertion, talk to a clinician before you start. For form, nothing on a screen beats a qualified coach watching you move.

02

Why it matters at any age

Six reasons, one per card. Open a card to read it. Every claim here is general on purpose: the useful research is about the direction of the effect, not a number you can put on your own body.

Muscle holds you upright, absorbs a stumble and carries the shopping. Left alone it thins with age. Research has associated resistance training with keeping it.

Bone gets denser when you load it, and tendons thicken. Studies have associated regular resistance work with stronger bone and steadier joints.

Leg and hip strength is what lets you catch yourself. Research has associated strength and balance work with a lower risk of falling.

Working muscle pulls sugar out of the blood. Studies have associated regular resistance training with better blood sugar control.

People often report steadier mood and easier sleep once training is routine, and research has associated exercise with both.

Standing from a low chair and carrying a bag upstairs are strength tasks. Training them now is what keeps them available later.

03

The five movement patterns

A movement pattern is a shape your body makes, not a particular exercise. There are five worth knowing. Cover all five in a week and you have trained almost everything you use.

Bending at the hips and knees together, torso staying fairly upright, to lower yourself and stand back up.

Every day
Standing up out of a low chair.
Beginner move
Box squat: sit back to a bench, touch it, stand up.
Cue
Sit back and down, and let your knees travel out over your toes.
Watch for
Knees drifting inward as you stand back up.

Bending mostly at the hips, back long and knees soft, to reach toward the floor and stand back up.

Every day
Picking a box up off the floor.
Beginner move
Romanian deadlift with a light dumbbell in each hand.
Cue
Push your hips back toward the wall behind you, chest long, no rounding.
Watch for
The back rounding as your hands travel down.

Moving a load away from you, either straight out in front or up overhead.

Every day
Pushing a heavy door open.
Beginner move
Incline push-up with your hands on a bench.
Cue
Ribs down, elbows at about forty five degrees, not flared straight out.
Watch for
Ribs flaring and the lower back arching to help.

Bringing a load toward you, from out in front or from overhead.

Every day
Hauling a stuck drawer open, or a bag out of the boot.
Beginner move
Single arm dumbbell row with your other hand on a bench.
Cue
Lead with the elbow and keep the shoulder down, away from your ear.
Watch for
The shoulder shrugging up toward your ear.

Holding a load and walking with it without letting your posture fall apart.

Every day
Carrying the shopping in from the car in one trip.
Beginner move
Farmer's carry: a weight in each hand, walk a set distance.
Cue
Stand tall, walk smooth, and do not lean away from the load.
Watch for
Leaning away from the weight instead of standing tall.

None of the five needs a gym to start. Every one has a version you can do with your own body weight, a chair, and a bit of floor.

            05

            How hard is hard enough

            Reps in reserve is the number of reps you could still have done when you stopped a set. Ten in reserve means you barely started. Zero means you could not have managed one more. Move the slider and read what that set would feel like.

            6
            Could do 10 more Could not do one more

            The beginner zone. The last rep slows down, and you still stop with two to four reps left in the tank. Form stays clean all the way through.

            1
            Nothing at all A big jump

            One more rep on a set or two. Small, boring, and it is the setting that keeps working for months.

            Progressive overload means giving your body slightly more to do over time: one more rep, one more set, a little more weight, or better control of the same weight. Without it there is no reason for anything to change. With too much of it at once, form goes first.

            06

            Spot the fault

            Six sets that have gone wrong. Read what is happening, pick the fault, and take the cue with you. Form comes before load every time, and sharp pain is always a stop.

            Set 1 of 6

            Answered 0 of 6.

            On a hinge, the back curves like the letter C as the hands reach toward the floor, and it curves further on every rep.

            Coming up out of a squat, the knees travel in toward each other while the feet stay flat and still.

            Three reps in, the lifter has not let a breath out, the face has gone red, and there are four reps still to go.

            At the bottom of every squat the lifter drops fast, rebounds off the bottom position, and springs straight back up.

            The lifter presses the weight up under control, then lets it fall straight back down to the start as fast as gravity will take it.

            First week back after years off. Rep one looks fine, rep three falls apart, and the last two look nothing like the first.

            None of these six is about being weak. Each one is a set going faster, heavier or longer than your form can hold, and that is a decision you can change on the very next rep.

            Your first week

            0 days, 0 of 5 moves.

            Two or three sessions a week is plenty to start, each one a full body session covering all five patterns. Leave at least a day between them: the getting stronger happens in the rest, not in the set.

            Which days will you train

            Nothing picked yet. Choose two or three days with a gap between them.

            Sleep and rest. Sore for a day or two after a new session is ordinary. Sharp pain, or pain in a joint rather than a muscle, is not: stop and get it looked at. Sleep is the cheapest thing on this list and the one that does the most.

            One move per pattern, done in the same session. Two or three sets of about six to twelve reps each, stopping with two to four reps left in the tank.

            My first week

            Nothing picked yet. Choose your days and one move per pattern, and this card fills itself in.

            How the session runs

            1. Warm up for five to ten minutes with easy movement.
            2. Two or three sets of each move, about six to twelve reps a set.
            3. Rest a minute or two between sets. Do not rush that part.
            4. Next time, the same session with a little more added.

            Stop each set with two to four reps left. Lower under control, breathe out on the effort, and stop altogether on sharp pain.
