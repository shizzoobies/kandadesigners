## How this complements the Oct 8 reel "AI drafts, you decide"
- **What the reel covers:** a 20-second, muted, text-led reel about a bike shop. It starts after the draft exists. It shows three tracked-change edits: tone, a wrong fact (a deal that had ended) and an over-promise (Friday). The lesson: the AI didn't know, so you fix it.
- **What this video covers:** the whole system that comes before the draft, which the reel never shows:
  - a one-page **voice notes** doc that gives the AI your facts and your voice
  - a **reusable prompt** that tells it to flag gaps instead of guessing
  - a three-point check (facts, prices, dates)
  - saving good replies as templates
  - privacy basics
  - which emails never go to AI
- **The one overlap** is the "check the draft" beat. It's framed as a checklist against the notes, not as surprise edits.

## 3. Narration script for Amy

About 970 words. At 150 wpm that's 6:28 of voice, plus about 40 s of demo holds and the 20 s end screen, for **about 7:28** in total.

**Words per beat:** B1 58 · B2 50 · B3 60 · B4 155 · B5 81 · B6 78 · B7 153 · B8 73 · B9 105 · B10 47 · B11 54 · B12 47.

The script has no em dashes. "K and A" and "Ninety-Day" are spelled out for the voice. It deliberately has no "I'm Amy" line, so the AI voice doesn't pose as a person.

**B1.** It's Monday morning. You open your inbox, and there they are. Twelve emails, and half of them ask the same things. Are you open Saturday? Can you do a custom order? How much is it? You know every answer by heart. You just don't have the time to type them all out, again, and still sound like yourself.

**B2.** In this video, I'll show you a simple way to get an AI assistant to write the first draft of those replies, in your voice, so all you have to do is check it and send. It works in whichever AI assistant you already use. This is K and A Performance, a web studio in Gainesville, Florida.

**B3.** Here's the plan. First, you'll write one short page about how you talk to customers. Then you'll save one prompt you can reuse. Then we'll try it on a real-looking email, and I'll show you the part most people skip: checking the draft before it goes out. We'll finish with privacy, and with the emails you should always answer yourself.

**B4.** Step one is your voice notes. This is a plain document, and it's the reason the replies will sound like you and not like a robot. Our example is a small bakery. Let's walk through it.
Start with how you greet people. This owner writes "Hi" and the customer's first name. Never "Dear valued customer."
Next, how you sign off. Here it's "Thanks so much," and then just "Rosa."
Then, the words you like to use, and the ones you never use. Rosa says "fresh" and "pickup." She never says "per our policy," because it sounds cold.
Now the most important part: your facts. Hours. How much notice you need for an order. How you handle allergies. And only the prices you're comfortable sharing in writing. If a price changes a lot, leave it out, and have the reply say you'll confirm it.
Keep it to one page. You can always add to it later.

**B5.** Step two is the prompt. This is the instruction you'll paste in every time. It says who you are, tells the assistant to follow your voice notes, and asks it to flag anything it isn't sure about instead of guessing. That last line matters. It's what stops the assistant from making up an answer.
Save this prompt somewhere easy, like a note on your computer. Then, each time, you paste three things: the prompt, your voice notes, and the customer's email.

**B6.** Let's try it. Here's an email from Jordan. Jordan would like three dozen cookies for pickup this Saturday, for a soccer party, and asks if a few can be made without nuts.
Copy the prompt, then the voice notes, then Jordan's email, and paste it all into your AI assistant.
And here's the draft. It opens with "Hi Jordan," it's short, it's warm, and it signs off the way Rosa does. Pretty close. But don't send it yet.

**B7, version A (use it if the real run contains slips):**
Step three is the one that protects you. Never send a reply you haven't read. Read it the way your customer will, and check three things: facts, prices, and dates.
Look here. The draft says the cookies will be ready at nine. Rosa's notes say Saturday pickups start at ten. That's a small mistake that turns into a customer standing at a locked door.
And look at this line. The draft promises the cookies will be nut free. Rosa's kitchen uses nuts, and her notes say so. She can bake a batch made without nuts and pack it separately, but she can't promise it's allergy safe. So we fix it, in her words.
AI assistants can sound very sure and still be wrong. The assistant only knows what you told it. Your name goes on the email, so you get the last word.
Now it's right. Now you can send it.

**B7, version B (use it if the real run is clean):**
Step three is the one that protects you. Never send a reply you haven't read. Read it the way your customer will, and check three things: facts, prices, and dates. Pickup time: ten to one, matches the notes. The nut line: made without nuts, packed separately, no promise it's allergy safe. That matches too. Price: three dozen at thirty dollars is ninety. Good. This one's right, and that's the voice notes doing their job. But check anyway, every single time. AI assistants can sound very sure and still be wrong, and your name goes on the email.

**B8.** Step four saves you even more time. When a reply comes out just right, save it. Keep a simple document of your best answers: the Saturday pickup reply, the custom order reply, the "yes, we have gluten free options" reply. Next time the same question shows up, start from your saved reply, or add it to your voice notes as an example. Over time, the drafts get closer to how you really write.

**B9.** A quick word on privacy. Treat anything you paste into an AI assistant like something you'd write on a postcard.
Don't paste in card numbers, passwords, or anyone's health information. If a customer's email includes something like that, delete it before you paste, or just answer that one yourself.
And take two minutes to look at your assistant's settings. Most have a privacy or data section where you can see how your chats are stored, and whether they're used to improve the service. Settings differ from one tool to the next, so check yours, and check your plan's terms if you're using it for work.

**B10.** Finally, know when not to use AI at all. If a customer is upset, write that reply yourself. They can tell. And anything that touches legal questions, medical questions, or money you might owe someone is a job for you, and maybe a professional, not a draft.

**B11.** Let's recap. One page of voice notes. One saved prompt. Paste in the prompt, your notes, and the email. Read every draft and check the facts, prices, and dates. Save your best replies. Keep private information out, and answer the hard emails yourself.
That's it. Try it on the next email you get today.

**B12.** If you'd like help setting up AI for a real job in your business, K and A Performance runs a paid, twelve-week coaching program called the Ninety-Day AI Launch. The link is in the description. And if this was useful, subscribe for a new walkthrough every week.

## 4. On-screen prompt and sample voice notes

**The reusable prompt (shown as C3):**
```
You help me reply to customer emails for my small business.
Follow my voice notes below for my greeting, sign-off, tone, words, and facts.

Rules:
1. Write a reply I can send after I check it. Keep it under 120 words.
2. Use only facts, times, prices, and policies from my voice notes.
3. If the email asks something my notes don't answer, don't guess.
   Put [CHECK: the question] where the answer belongs.
4. Never promise anything my notes don't say we can do.
5. End with my sign-off.

My voice notes:
[paste your voice notes here]

The customer's email:
[paste the email here]
```

**Sample voice notes (shown as C2).** The header carries a small-caps line: `SAMPLE BUSINESS · NOT A REAL BAKERY`.
```
Voice notes | Rosa's bakery

Greeting: "Hi" and the customer's first name.
Sign-off: "Thanks so much," then "Rosa" on its own line.
Tone: warm, short, cheerful. Two or three short paragraphs. One exclamation point at most.
Words I use: fresh, pickup, batch, happy to.
Words I never use: "per our policy", "valued customer", "unfortunately"
  (say what we can do instead).

Hours: Tue to Fri 7 AM to 3 PM. Saturday pickups 10 AM to 1 PM. Closed Sun and Mon.

Payment: card or cash at pickup. Never ask for card numbers by email.
Always answer myself: complaints, refunds, wholesale, any allergy question
  beyond the line above.
```

## 6. Facts check

Every privacy claim in the script is kept general. Here's what supports each one:
- **"Don't paste card numbers."** PCI DSS Requirement 4.2.2 prohibits sending unprotected card numbers (PANs) over end-user messaging such as email and chat ([PCI SSC FAQ](https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/faqs/1085/), [SecurityMetrics](https://www.securitymetrics.com/blog/sending-credit-card-info-over-email)). The same principle backs the "never ask for card numbers by email" line in the notes.
- **"Most have a privacy or data section… whether chats are used to improve the service."** Checked for the three biggest consumer assistants:
  - one has a "Data controls" setting to turn off model improvement ([OpenAI Help](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/7730893-data-controls-faq))
  - one lets users choose, in Privacy Settings, whether chats train models ([Anthropic, Aug 2025 consumer terms update](https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms), [privacy center](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/7996868-is-my-data-used-for-model-training))
  - one has an activity setting and says human reviewers may see some chats, and to avoid entering confidential information ([Gemini Apps Privacy Hub](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961?hl=en))

  None of these is named on screen or in the narration.
- **"Check your plan's terms if you're using it for work."** True as general advice, because business and consumer plans handle data differently. No specific claim is made.
- **Health information, passwords, complaints, legal and medical.** Framed as common-sense advice, not as legal claims. No HIPAA claim is made.
- **Offer claims.** "Paid, twelve-week coaching program" matches `src/pages/ai-launch.astro` (a $2,000 program over twelve weeks). No price is said in the video.
- **Demo numbers.** All fictional and labeled as a sample business: $30 a dozen, $90 total, the hours. No statistics, no time savings, no promised results.
